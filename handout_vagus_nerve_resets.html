Vagus Nerve Resets

Simple, evidence-based practices that activate the parasympathetic nervous system and signal safety to the body

Your nervous system can learn to brake. These practices teach it how.
The vagus nerve is the master controller of your parasympathetic nervous system, the biological pathway that builds your capacity to rest, digest, and heal. Every practice below directly stimulates vagal pathways and strengthens the brake that counters the chronic activation helpers carry. None require equipment, privacy, or more than a few minutes. Most can be done at a desk, in a car, or on a break. The more consistently you use them, the more readily your nervous system returns to baseline on its own.
Breathing Resets
01
Cyclic Sigh
Physiological Sigh
Inhale fully through the nose, then take a second short sniff on top to fully inflate the lungs. Then release a long, slow exhale through the mouth. Repeat 1 to 3 times.

This is also called the physiological sigh, the same pattern your body does involuntarily during sleep and stress to re-inflate collapsed alveoli and offload CO2. The intentional version is the fastest studied intervention for reducing acute physiological stress. A 2023 Stanford clinical study found cyclic sighing outperformed meditation and box breathing for real-time stress reduction.
One to three repetitions is enough. You will often feel the shift immediately, a softening in the chest or a spontaneous deeper breath that follows.
02
Extended Exhale Breathing
Sustained Calm
Inhale for 4 counts, exhale slowly for 6 to 8 counts. Repeat for 2 to 5 minutes.

Long exhales activate the vagal brake more strongly than inhales do. Every extended exhale is a direct signal to the nervous system that the threat has passed and it is safe to down-regulate. Box breathing (4 counts in, hold 4, out 4, hold 4) is a related pattern that works especially well for people who find the extended exhale too slow.
Even 60 seconds of extended exhale breathing measurably reduces heart rate and cortisol. You do not need a full session for a meaningful effect.
Physical Resets
03
Cold Water
Dive Reflex
Splash cold water on your face, or hold your wrists under cold running water for 30 to 60 seconds. For a stronger reset, hold your breath and submerge your face in a bowl of cold water for 15 to 30 seconds.

Cold water to the face triggers the mammalian dive reflex, a hardwired vagal response that immediately slows heart rate and activates the parasympathetic system. The wrist version is faster and more discreet, making it practical between shifts or in a break room.
The colder the water, the stronger the reflex. Even cool water works, cold enough to feel the shock is the threshold.
04
Humming and Vocal Toning
Vocal Vagal
Hum from your chest for 1 to 2 minutes, feeling the vibration move through the throat and sternum. Try sustained toning on an "OM," "AH," or "MMM" sound. Even quiet humming to yourself counts.

The vagus nerve runs directly through the throat and larynx. Vocal vibration is a direct physical massage of the nerve itself. This is distinct from gargling, humming engages the deeper resonance of the chest and diaphragm in addition to the throat.
Singing in the car on the way home is a legitimate vagal reset. So is humming while paperwork.
05
Gargling
Throat Activation
Gargle vigorously with water for 30 to 60 seconds. Do this once or twice. The stronger the gargle, the more the vagal branches in the throat are activated.

Gargling directly stimulates the pharyngeal branch of the vagus nerve at the back of the throat, a different mechanism than humming. Some helpers use it as a morning reset, others use it when they feel themselves going into sympathetic overdrive and need a quick intervention that is easy to do in a restroom.
Gargling until your eyes water slightly is a sign you are activating the right tissue. That is a vagal reflex, not a problem.
06
Vagal Massage
Touch-Based
Gently massage down the sides of your neck along the carotid area, the front of the throat, and behind the ears. Slow, firm pressure. 1 to 2 minutes.

Also try the ear and tragus, squeeze and roll the small cartilage flap at the entrance of your ear canal between your thumb and forefinger. The tragus and concha bowl are where the vagus nerve surfaces directly on the skin, making this one of the most targeted physical vagal stimulation points available outside a clinical setting.
Auricular vagus nerve stimulation is an active area of clinical research. The ear is the only place on the external body where you can directly access vagal tissue.
07
Gentle Movement
Somatic
Slow neck rolls, child's pose, gentle spinal twists, or shaking the hands and arms loose. Even a brief walk at a slow pace activates vagal pathways through rhythmic movement and proprioception.

Movement signals the body that it is not frozen, it interrupts the immobility response that chronic stress can trigger. The key word is slow and gentle. Intense exercise activates the sympathetic system first. The vagal benefit of movement comes from rhythm and ease, not effort.
A 5-minute slow walk after a difficult encounter with someone you serve is not a luxury. It is nervous system first aid.
Relational and Sensory Resets
08
Eye Gaze Reset
Ocular Reset
Cover one eye with your hand. With the other eye, look slowly up and to the outer corner. Hold that gaze position until you feel a spontaneous yawn, sigh, or softening, which is the vagal release signal. This typically takes 30 seconds to 2 minutes. Repeat on the other side.

Moving the eyes into the upper outer corners tenses and then releases muscles near the brainstem, directly signaling the vagus nerve to shift the body into parasympathetic dominance. Covering one eye reduces visual processing load, deepening the effect. This is the Rosenberg ocular reset, drawn from Stanley Rosenberg's work on the vagus nerve.
Wait for the yawn or sigh, that is the signal the reset is complete, not the clock. If it takes longer than 2 minutes, your nervous system may need more activation first.
09
Social Engagement
Polyvagal
A genuine moment of warm eye contact, a real laugh, a brief connection with another person where you both feel seen. Petting an animal counts. So does a hug that lasts more than 6 seconds.

Polyvagal theory places social engagement at the top of the autonomic hierarchy. The ventral vagal system, the newest and most human part of the vagus nerve, is activated specifically through connection, warmth, and safety cues from another regulated nervous system. This is not soft science. It is the physiological mechanism behind why isolation is so damaging to health and why community is medicine.
For helpers specifically: you spend all day in social engagement that is one-directional and high-stakes. The reset is mutual, low-stakes, warm connection, where you are also received, not only giving.

✦ A Note for Helpers
You have likely spent years managing others' emergencies while your own nervous system remained in high alert. Your body may have forgotten what safety feels like. These practices are not luxuries, they are medicine. They build your nervous system's capacity to return to safety. They teach your nervous system that it is safe to rest, safe to feel, and safe to release what you have been carrying.

Your healing is your greatest gift to those you serve.
